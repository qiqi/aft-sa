\begin{abstract}
The envelope $e^N$ method predicts natural transition from three inputs: the
shape of the boundary-layer profile, the momentum-thickness Reynolds number,
and the freestream turbulence intensity. The first two are integrals across
the boundary layer, and the amplification factor $N$ is an integral along it.
An unstructured Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) solver evaluates only
local quantities and cannot apply the method directly. The model described
here predicts natural transition with local quantities, inside the one-equation
Spalart--Allmaras (SA) turbulence model. Where the SA variable $\tilde\nu$ is
below the molecular viscosity $\nu$, the eddy viscosity of SA is negligible,
and $\ln\tilde\nu$ is used as $N$. Its growth rate is a function of two
dimensionless indicators of the profile shape, a vorticity fraction and an
accumulated inflection. Both are formed from the velocity, its first and
second derivatives, and the wall distance at a point. Its growth begins where
a vorticity Reynolds number exceeds a threshold. Its freestream value is a
function of the turbulence intensity. Near
$\tilde\nu=\nu$ the production changes from the amplification source to the
production of SA. One equation therefore has two regimes: the laminar growth
of a disturbance, and the turbulence that follows it. The constants of the
amplification are determined by three results of stability theory: the growth
rate of a free shear layer, the linear-stability onset of the Falkner--Skan
family, and the Blasius $e^N$ envelope. The model is computed on a
zero-pressure-gradient flat plate, the NLF(1)-0416 and Eppler 387 airfoils, a
two-element section, a circular cylinder through the drag crisis, the Daedalus
human-powered-aircraft wing, and a 6:1 prolate spheroid, and compared with
experimental data and with other transition models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\laozi[7.5pt]{40}

A laminar boundary layer in a stream of low turbulence intensity does not
turn turbulent all at once. It first becomes unstable to small-amplitude
two-dimensional waves, the
Tollmien--Schlichting (TS) waves~\cite{mack_1977,schmid_henningson_2001}. Waves
of different frequencies grow at different rates. The envelope, the largest
amplitude over all frequencies, grows nearly exponentially along the surface,
by a factor of several thousand. The distance that this growth takes determines
where transition occurs. Transition itself takes a much shorter distance: three-dimensional instabilities become
dominant, and turbulent spots form, spread, and
merge~\cite{schubauer_klebanoff_1956}. The location of transition changes the
forces on a body. On the
natural-laminar-flow airfoil of Section~III.B, a computation that assumes
transition at the leading edge gives $1.6$ to $2.5$ times the drag of a
computation in which the model determines the transition location.

The envelope $e^N$ method represents the growth of the envelope by one number, the amplification factor $N$ \cite{smith_gamberoni_1956,vaningen_2008}. It is the logarithm of
the factor by which the envelope has grown along the boundary layer. Transition is declared
where $N$ reaches $N_\mathrm{crit}$, and $N_\mathrm{crit}$ is a function of the freestream turbulence intensity~\cite{mack_1977}. Drela and Giles reduced the stability
calculation behind $N$ to two functions of the shape factor $H$ \cite{drela_giles_1987}. The shape factor is the ratio of the
displacement thickness to the momentum thickness, one dimensionless number for the shape of the
boundary-layer profile. The envelope does not grow until the momentum-thickness Reynolds number $Re_\theta$ passes a critical
value $Re_{\theta0}(H)$. Past it, $N$ grows at a rate $dN/dRe_\theta$ that depends on $H$ alone. The method therefore does three jobs,
and each job has its own input:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item How fast the disturbance grows. The input is the shape of the boundary-layer profile, through $H$.
\item When the growth starts. The input is $Re_\theta$, compared with a threshold that also depends on $H$.
\item How far the disturbance must grow. The input is the freestream turbulence intensity.
\end{enumerate}

A general RANS solver cannot apply this method directly. Both $H$ and $Re_\theta$ are integrals across the boundary layer, and $N$ is an integral along it. An unstructured solver can evaluate only
local quantities at each grid point: the flow variables, their gradients, and
the distance to the nearest wall.

The model described here does the three jobs with local quantities, inside
the Spalart--Allmaras (SA) equation~\cite{spalart_allmaras_1992}. SA
transports one field, $\tilde\nu$. Where $\chi=\tilde\nu/\nu$ is below one,
the resulting eddy viscosity is negligible. The baseline model leaves this
range ``empty''. Here $\ln\tilde\nu$ fills it, in the role of $N$. Its starting level, the
freestream value, is a function of the freestream turbulence intensity. Its growth
begins where a local Reynolds number, formed from the vorticity and the wall
distance, exceeds a threshold. Its growth rate depends only on the local shape of
the boundary-layer profile. Near $\chi=1$ the production switches to that of baseline SA. One
equation therefore has two regimes: the laminar growth of a disturbance, and
the turbulence that follows it. The model is called SA-AI\footnote{``AI'' here is not the other kind making headlines in the 2020s.}, Spalart--Allmaras with
Autogenous Inception: the turbulence variable carries the inception of its
own turbulence.

SA-AI is a transition-aware RANS model for natural, Tollmien--Schlichting transition at low Mach number.
Bypass transition ($Tu\gtrsim1\%$), crossflow instability, and
compressibility effects are not modeled.

Section~\ref{sec:model} builds the model one piece at a time and ends with
its constants. Each piece is the simplest form found to do its job, and no
piece has been refined beyond that. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports what it computes on a flat
plate, two airfoils, a two-element section, a circular cylinder through the
drag crisis, the Daedalus wing, and a 6:1 prolate spheroid.
Section~\ref{sec:envelope} describes what the model computes in five
situations beyond those cases: a rotating frame, a curved wall, a swept
attachment line, a wall with suction, and the wake of another surface.


\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Transport Equation and Blended Sources}

\laozi[7.6pt]{11}

SA's eddy viscosity is $\rho\tilde\nu f_{v1}(\chi)$, with
$f_{v1}=\chi^3/(\chi^3+c_{v1}^3)$ and $c_{v1}=7.1$. At $\chi=1$ that is about
$0.3\%$ of $\rho\tilde\nu$. Below $\chi\approx1$ the eddy viscosity is negligible whatever the value of $\tilde\nu$. This is the empty range.

In that empty range, a source proportional to $\tilde\nu$ makes $\tilde\nu$
grow exponentially along a particle path. When $\tilde\nu\ll\nu$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{D\ln\tilde\nu}{Dt} = a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega ,
  \label{w:lngrowth}
\end{equation}
and $\ln\tilde\nu$ accumulates as $N$ does, at the rate $a\,\sigma_{Re}$ on
the time scale $1/\omega$, the inverse of the vorticity magnitude. Three quantities each do one of the three jobs of the $e^N$ method listed in the Introduction. The rate $a$ determines how fast the
disturbance grows. It is a dimensionless number that depends only on the shape of the boundary-layer profile. The gate $\sigma_{Re}$ determines when the growth starts. A gate is a factor
between zero and one that multiplies a term: at zero the gate is shut and the
term vanishes, at one it is open and the term acts in full. This gate,
$0\le\sigma_{Re}\le1$, opens as a local Reynolds number exceeds a threshold.
Every $\sigma$ with a subscript in this paper is such a factor; the $\sigma$
without a subscript is the SA diffusion constant. The freestream value $\chi_\infty$ determines how far the disturbance must grow: $\ln\tilde\nu$ increases from that value
until $\chi$ reaches one.

The full model is SA with that source and four adjustments: a reduced
molecular diffusion, a handover in the production, a matching weight on the
destruction, and a bypass in the eddy viscosity. It is stated in full in this
subsection, for reference. The subsections that follow reach it from
Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} one step at a time. Sections~II.B and~II.C define $a$
and $\sigma_{Re}$, which completes Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth}. Section~II.D adds
the molecular diffusion of $\tilde\nu$. Section~II.E adds the handover to SA.
Section~II.G writes the indicators for a three-dimensional flow.
Section~II.F turns to the momentum equation, which the model affects only
through the eddy viscosity:
\begin{gather}
  \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\mathbf u) = 0,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial(\rho\mathbf u)}{\partial t}
    + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\,\mathbf u\!\otimes\!\mathbf u)
    = -\nabla p + \nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol\tau, \notag\\[3pt]
  \boldsymbol\tau = (\mu+\mu_t)\big[\nabla\mathbf u
    + (\nabla\mathbf u)^{\!\top}
    - \tfrac23(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf u)\,\mathbf I\big],
  \qquad
  \mu_t = \big[(1-\sigma_{v1})\,f_{v1}(\chi)+\sigma_{v1}\big]\,\rho\tilde\nu,
  \label{w:ns}
\end{gather}
with $\sigma_{v1}$ the lifted-layer bypass weight of Eq.~\eqref{w:fv1bypass}. The
working variable is transported by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{D\tilde\nu}{Dt} = P - D
  + \frac{1}{\sigma}\nabla\!\cdot\!\big[(c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\,\nu+\tilde\nu)\,\nabla\tilde\nu\big]
  + \frac{c_{b2}}{\sigma}\,|\nabla\tilde\nu|^2,
  \label{w:sa}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  P = \max\!\big[\,(1-\sigma_P)\,a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega\,\tilde\nu,\;\;\sigma_P\,P_\mathrm{SA}\,\big],
  \qquad
  D = \sigma_D\,D_\mathrm{SA} .
  \label{w:blend}
\end{equation}
The rate $a$ is defined by Eq.~\eqref{w:a}, the gate $\sigma_{Re}$ by Eq.~\eqref{w:rate}, and the
weights $\sigma_P$ and $\sigma_D$ by Eq.~\eqref{w:sigma}.
The production $P$ is the amplification source,
$P_\mathrm{AI}=a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega\,\tilde\nu$, while $\chi<1$, the laminar
regime, and SA's own production once $\sigma_P$ has reached one, the
turbulent regime. The destruction is SA's destruction multiplied by $\sigma_D$. The molecular viscosity in the diffusion term of
$\tilde\nu$ is multiplied by $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$. In the eddy viscosity, $f_{v1}$ is
replaced by $(1-\sigma_{v1})f_{v1}+\sigma_{v1}$, with $\sigma_{v1}$ the bypass weight.
Note that baseline SA is recovered exactly by setting $\sigma_P\!\equiv\!1$
(and with it $\sigma_D\!=\!1$), $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\!=\!1$, and
$\sigma_{v1}\!\equiv\!0$. Note also that Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} is recovered
by setting $\sigma_P\!=\!0$ and $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\!=\!0$ where
$\chi\ll1$: the destruction and the remaining diffusion terms are then of
second order in $\tilde\nu$. The SA closure functions are unchanged:
\begin{gather}
  P_\mathrm{SA} = c_{b1}\tilde S\,\tilde\nu, \qquad
  D_\mathrm{SA} = c_{w1}f_w\!\left(\frac{\tilde\nu}{d}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad
  \tilde S = \omega + \frac{\tilde\nu}{\kappa^2 d^2}\,f_{v2}, \qquad
  f_{v2} = 1 - \frac{\chi}{1+\chi f_{v1}}, \notag\\[3pt]
  f_{v1} = \frac{\chi^3}{\chi^3+c_{v1}^3}, \qquad
  f_w = g\!\left(\frac{1+c_{w3}^6}{g^6+c_{w3}^6}\right)^{\!1/6}, \qquad
  g = r + c_{w2}(r^6-r), \qquad
  r = \min\!\left(\frac{\tilde\nu}{\tilde S\,\kappa^2 d^2},\,10\right).
  \label{w:closure}
\end{gather}
The
non-negative $\tilde S$ modification and the negative-$\tilde\nu$
safeguards of Allmaras et al.~\cite{allmaras_2012} are retained, with one
consistency requirement: the negative-branch diffusion factor $f_n$ is
formed with the scaled coefficient $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\,\nu$, which
keeps the total diffusivity positive,
$c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\nu+f_n\tilde\nu>0$.
The boundary conditions are also those of SA. At a wall $\tilde\nu=0$. In the
free stream $\tilde\nu=\chi_\infty\nu$, with $\chi_\infty$ given in
Section~III.A. The handover weights are
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_P = \sigma_t(\chi;\tau), \qquad
  \sigma_t(\chi;\tau) = \max\!\left[\,1-e^{-(\chi-1)/\tau},\;0\right], \qquad
  \sigma_D = 1-\frac{c_{b1}}{\kappa^2c_{w1}}\,(1-\sigma_P).
  \label{w:sigma}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Local Indicators of the Boundary-Layer Profile Shape}

\laozi[7.6pt]{42}

The rate $a$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth}, and the threshold at which the gate
$\sigma_{Re}$ opens, are functions of the shape of the boundary-layer profile.
The model must therefore determine that shape from local quantities.

SA-AI assumes that the shapes of laminar boundary-layer profiles form a
\textbf{one}-parameter family. The shape of a profile is the velocity divided
by the edge velocity, as a function of the wall distance divided by the
momentum thickness. It is a whole curve, and two boundary layers could in
principle differ in any part of it. The assumption is that in practice they
do not: a single number selects the curve, and once that number is known the
rest of the shape is known. The Falkner--Skan profiles, selected by $\beta$,
are such a family. The assumption is an approximation, and it holds best where
the pressure gradient changes slowly along the surface. It is the assumption
behind one-parameter integral methods such as that of Thwaites
\cite{thwaites_1949}, and behind the envelope $e^N$ method
\cite{drela_giles_1987}, where the number is the shape factor $H$ and
$dN/dRe_\theta$ is a function of $H$ alone. However, SA-AI cannot use $H$,
which is an integral across the boundary layer and cannot be determined from the values and
derivatives of the velocity at a point. A local quantity must replace $H$.

But one local quantity is not enough, because any local quantity changes across the boundary layer. Consider the ratio of $d\,u'$ to $u$, with $u$ the velocity, $u'$ its
wall-normal derivative, and $d$ the distance to the wall. It is one at the
wall and zero at the edge of every attached boundary layer. Every value in between occurs for every profile shape, at some wall distance. Identifying the shape from local quantities requires \textbf{two} independent ratios, because two numbers are unknown at a point. One is the
number that parameterizes the shape. The other is the wall-normal coordinate of the point: its wall distance
divided by the momentum thickness.

Two independent ratios need \textbf{three} local quantities of the same dimension, because two quantities form only one ratio. At a point, a solver
can evaluate $u$, $u'$, and the second derivative $u''$. Multiplied by powers
of $d$, they become the terms of the Taylor series over the distance to the
wall: $u$, $d\,u'$, and $\tfrac12 d^2u''$, all with the dimension of velocity.

And three is sufficient. In a thin quasi-two-dimensional boundary layer these three
velocity scales determine the Taylor expansion of the profile to second order
about any wall-normal point. Normalized by their
common magnitude, the local velocity scale
$U_\mathrm{loc}=\sqrt{u^2+(du')^2+(\tfrac12 d^2u'')^2}$, they define a point on the unit sphere of directions
\begin{equation}
  (X,Y,Z) = \big(u,\;d\,u',\;\tfrac12 d^2u''\big),
  \qquad
  (\hat X,\hat Y,\hat Z) = (X,Y,Z)\big/U_\mathrm{loc},
  \label{w:ind}
\end{equation}
so unhatted symbols have the dimension of velocity and hatted ones are the
direction cosines on the unit sphere. The normalization removes the
magnitude and keeps the direction of $(X,Y,Z)$. Any ratio of the three terms,
such as $Y/X$ or $Z/X$, can be computed from that direction. The direction is
used in place of two chosen ratios because each of the three terms vanishes
somewhere in a flow, so none can be the denominator everywhere.
Figure~\ref{f:sphere} plots boundary-layer profiles of the Falkner--Skan family
\cite{falkner_skan_1931} on the sphere. Each profile maps to
a curve, parameterized by the wall distance from the wall to the edge of the boundary layer. Every profile with nonzero wall shear starts at a wall point,
$\hat X=\hat Y$, $\hat Z=0$, because $u\approx u'd\gg u''d^2$ next to the
wall. Every profile ends at a velocity pole, $\hat X=\pm1$: toward the edge of the boundary layer $u'$ and $u''$ decay at least
exponentially with $d$, so $Y$ and $Z$ vanish.
Between their end points no two curves cross. A point on the sphere therefore
corresponds to exactly one profile shape and one wall-normal coordinate, the two unknowns.

The model is built on one feature of Fig.~\ref{f:sphere}. Close to the wall
every curve lies on the same great circle, whatever the pressure gradient:
favorable, zero, adverse, or strong enough to reverse the flow at the wall.
Each curve leaves its wall point along that circle, which is the image of every parabola through the wall: any $u=c_1d+c_2d^2$ satisfies
$\hat Y=\hat X+\hat Z$ at every wall distance, shear equals velocity plus
curvature. What distinguishes one profile from another is its departure from
that circle. One indicator measures the departure:
\begin{equation}
  \hat I = \hat Y - \hat X - \hat Z
  \qquad\text{(accumulated inflection)}.
  \label{w:ihat}
\end{equation}
It is zero on the circle. Its name comes from the unnormalized form,
\begin{equation}
  I(d) \equiv U_\mathrm{loc}\,\hat I = d\,u'-u-\tfrac12 d^2u''
  = -\int_0^d \tfrac12\,s^2\,u'''(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .
  \label{w:integral}
\end{equation}
The accumulated inflection is the integral of $-u'''$ from the wall to the
point, weighted by $\tfrac12 s^2$. It measures how much the curvature $u''$
has decreased between the wall and the point. It is zero for a parabola,
whose curvature is constant. The same three terms have a second interpretation. The second-order Taylor expansion of the profile at the point, extrapolated
to the wall, gives a wall velocity of $X-Y+Z=-I$. The no-slip condition
requires zero. The accumulated inflection is the true wall velocity minus the
extrapolated one. The circle is the line of zero accumulated inflection: no
AI, in a second sense of the abbreviation.

Away from the wall the profiles of Fig.~\ref{f:sphere} differ in the maximum of
$\hat I$. The strongly favorable profile, $\beta=+0.30$, has a maximum $\hat I$ of only $0.007$ and is
negative farther from the wall. For the Blasius profile the maximum is $0.14$.
In an adverse pressure gradient the maximum is larger. The separated profile, the reversed-flow solution
of Stewartson~\cite{stewartson_1954}, starts at the antipodal wall point, with velocity and shear both negative, and has the largest maximum of all.


\subsection{Amplification Rate and Onset Gate Built from the Indicators}

\laozi[7.6pt]{64}

The amplification rate $a$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} is defined first. It is a
single dimensionless number at every point of the sphere, and it measures how
unstable a profile of that shape is at that wall distance.
Figure~\ref{f:sphere} shows where linear stability theory places the
instability. The amplification of an Orr--Sommerfeld wave is a spatial rate,
with the dimension of inverse length. It is usually made dimensionless with
the boundary-layer thickness, which is not available at a point.
Equation~\eqref{w:lngrowth} supplies a local length in its place: a steady
disturbance grows in space at the rate $a\,\omega/u$, so the length is
$u/\omega$. The thick part of each curve is the band of wall distances where
the Reynolds-stress production of the most amplified wave, scaled with
$u/\omega$, exceeds half its peak. The objective is
a simple function on the sphere that is large where those bands lie.

The accumulated inflection $\hat I$ is the starting point for modeling the
amplification rate. A profile with no inflection point is stable to inviscid
disturbances, by Rayleigh's theorem~\cite{schmid_henningson_2001}, and a
parabola has none. The great circle $\hat I=0$, the image of every parabola
through the wall, is the neutral line of the model. On the side where
inflection has accumulated, $\hat I$ is positive, and every band lies there.
The accumulated inflection cannot be the rate by itself: a flow and its
reverse, $u\to-u$, are equally unstable, but $\hat I$ changes sign under the
reversal. The simplest way to respect this symmetry is to multiply $\hat I$ by a second indicator that also changes sign under $u\to-u$. An obvious candidate is the shear cosine $\hat Y$, but it fails for a shear layer far from the wall. The curvature scale $Z$ is proportional to $d^2$. In
such a shear layer $Z$ dominates $U_\mathrm{loc}$, and $\hat Y$ tends to zero
however strong the shear.

The vorticity fraction changes sign under $u\to-u$ and does not contain $Z$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat\Omega = \frac{\hat Y}{\sqrt{\hat X^2+\hat Y^2}}
  = \frac{Y}{\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}}
  \qquad\text{(vorticity fraction)}.
  \label{w:omhat}
\end{equation}
It is the fraction of shear in the combined magnitude of velocity and shear.
It is zero in a uniform stream and at the center of a jet or a wake. It
approaches one in a detached shear layer, at any distance from the wall. The
product $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is unchanged under $u\to-u$.

The contours of $\hat\Omega\hat I$ in Fig.~\ref{f:sphere} follow the thick
bands. The product is positive in every band, and across the Falkner--Skan
family it grows with the adverse pressure gradient. For $\beta\ge\tfrac12$ it
is never positive, because $\hat I$ is never positive there while
$\hat\Omega$ is. Its maximum is below $0.005$ on the stable profile,
$\beta=+0.30$, is $0.08$ on the Blasius profile, and reaches $0.66$ on the
separated one. On every unstable profile the maximum lies inside the band. Within every profile the product is zero where either indicator is
zero: on the great circle $\hat I=0$, and in the free stream, where the shear
vanishes and $\hat\Omega$ with it.

The amplification rate of SA-AI, $a$ in Eqs.~\eqref{w:lngrowth}
and~\eqref{w:blend}, is this product, clipped to the interval from zero to
one and multiplied by $a_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}
  a = a_{\max}\,\operatorname{clip}\!\big\langle\,\hat\Omega\,\hat I\,\big\rangle_{0}^{1} .
  \label{w:a}
\end{equation}
The clip
sets the rate to zero wherever the product is not positive, so $\tilde\nu$
does not grow at the wall or in the free stream. The ceiling $a_{\max}$ is the rate of the free shear
layer, the most unstable profile of all. The fastest wave on a
hyperbolic-tangent shear layer grows at $0.1897$ of the peak vorticity
\cite{michalke_1964}, which gives $a_{\max}=0.19$. The rate of any other
profile is $a_{\max}$ multiplied by $\hat\Omega\hat I$.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports what the model computes with this rate.

The rate $a$ does not depend on the viscosity, just as $dN/dRe_\theta$ does
not in the $e^N$ method. Viscosity appears only in the onset gate,
$\sigma_{Re}$ in Eqs.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} and~\eqref{w:blend}. The local
Reynolds number is the vorticity Reynolds number $Re_\Omega=d^2\omega/\nu$.
The gate is a standard sigmoid in the relative excess of $Re_\Omega$ over a
threshold $Re_\Omega^{\mathrm c}$:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{Re} = \tfrac12\big[1+\tanh(\zeta/w)\big],
  \qquad
  \zeta = \frac{Re_\Omega-Re_\Omega^{\mathrm c}}{Re_\Omega^{\mathrm c}} .
  \label{w:rate}
\end{equation}
It is one half at the threshold. The constant $w$ is the relative width of
the rise. With $w=0.35$ the gate is $0.003$ at $Re_\Omega=0$ and $0.997$ at
twice the threshold. The threshold depends on the same shape number
$\hat\Omega\hat I$, as $Re_{\theta0}$ depends on $H$.

The threshold has one job: to start the growth where the $e^N$ method starts
it, at the critical Reynolds number $Re_{\theta0}(H)$. Figure~\ref{f:graze} shows
how. A boundary layer at one station traces a curve in the plane of
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ and $Re_\Omega$, one point for each wall distance. Each
colored curve in the figure is one Falkner--Skan profile. Increasing
$Re_\theta$ without changing the profile shape multiplies $Re_\Omega$ by the
same factor at every wall distance. On the logarithmic scale of the figure the
whole curve moves up without changing its form. The gate
opens at the first wall distance where the curve reaches the threshold. For
the growth to start at $Re_{\theta0}$, the curve at that Reynolds number must
touch the threshold at one point and lie below it everywhere else. The same must
hold for every profile shape, each taken at its own critical Reynolds number,
where the boundary layer has just become thick enough for a disturbance to
grow. The threshold is therefore the upper bound of all those curves, one that touches each of them.

The curves in Fig.~\ref{f:graze} are drawn at those critical Reynolds
numbers, for nine attached Falkner--Skan profiles from $\beta=+0.15$ to
incipient separation. Each curve starts at the
lower left, next to the wall, where $\hat\Omega\hat I$ and $Re_\Omega$ are both
small. Away from the wall it rises to the right, turns back at the wall
distance where $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is largest, and returns to the left as
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ decreases toward the edge of the boundary layer. The turning points mark this upper bound. They run from the upper left to the lower right. A
favorable profile becomes critical at a large $Re_\Omega$, about $2000$ at
$\beta=+0.15$, and has a small $\hat\Omega\hat I$. A profile near separation
becomes critical at $Re_\Omega\approx170$ and has a large $\hat\Omega\hat I$. On the logarithmic scales of the figure the upper bound is level on the left, level again on the
right at about one fifteenth of that value, and falls from one level to the other between
$\hat\Omega\hat I\approx0.03$ and $0.2$.

The following function, with three constants, is level at both ends and
decreases in between:
\begin{equation}
  Re_\Omega^{\mathrm c}(\hat\Omega\hat I) = \con\,\operatorname{softmin}_2\!\Big(C,\;
      A + B\,\langle\hat\Omega\hat I\rangle_+^{-2}\Big),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{softmin}_2(x_1,x_2)=\frac{x_1x_2}{\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}},
  \qquad \langle x\rangle_+=\max(x,0).
  \label{w:onset}
\end{equation}
The constant $C$ is the level on the left, where $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is small
and the second argument is large. The sum in the second argument is a smooth
maximum of its two terms, and it tends to $A$, the level on the right, where
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ is large. The constant $B$ determines the range of $\hat\Omega\hat I$ over which the
threshold decreases from $C$ to $A$: the sum equals $C$ near $\hat\Omega\hat I=\sqrt{B/C}$ and is twice $A$ at
$\hat\Omega\hat I=\sqrt{B/A}$. With $\con=1$ and $(C,A,B)=(2600,\,175,\,2)$ the
function is the dashed line of Fig.~\ref{f:graze}.

The constants $(C,A,B)$ were fitted for Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth}, in which the
growth begins as soon as the gate opens. Section~II.A noted that the full
equation reduces to Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} only when
$c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}=0$. With $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}>0$, diffusion carries
$\tilde\nu$ away from the band where it is produced and removes it at the
wall, where $\tilde\nu$ is zero, so the net growth begins later than the gate
opens. The threshold is lowered to compensate, through the onset factor $\con$ in Eq.~\eqref{w:onset}.

\subsection{Laminar Diffusion and the Onset Factor}

\laozi[7.6pt]{77}

With $a$ and $\sigma_{Re}$ defined, Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} is complete. Where
$\chi\ll1$ the transport equation, Eq.~\eqref{w:sa}, contains one more term:
the molecular diffusion of $\tilde\nu$. This subsection determines the two constants connected with it: the factor
$c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$, which reduces the diffusion, and the onset factor
$\con$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:onset}.

The factor $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}<1$ multiplies the molecular viscosity in the
diffusion term of Eq.~\eqref{w:sa} only. The mean-flow viscosity, the SA
production and destruction, and $\chi=\tilde\nu/\nu$, formed with the true
$\nu$, are unchanged. The effect of $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$ is measured on the Blasius boundary layer.
Where $\chi\ll1$, Eq.~\eqref{w:sa} in the boundary-layer approximation is
\begin{equation}
  u\,\frac{\partial\tilde\nu}{\partial x}+v\,\frac{\partial\tilde\nu}{\partial y}
  = a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega\,\tilde\nu
  + \frac{c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\,\nu}{\sigma}\,
    \frac{\partial^2\tilde\nu}{\partial y^2},
  \label{w:lamdiff}
\end{equation}
with $x$ along the plate and $y$ normal to it. This is
Eq.~\eqref{w:lngrowth} with the laminar diffusion added. The table below lists
its solution on the Blasius velocity field $(u,v)$, marched downstream from the
leading edge, with $\tilde\nu=0$ at the wall and $\tilde\nu=\chi_\infty\nu$ at
the leading edge and in the free stream. The equation is linear in
$\tilde\nu$, so the amplification factor
\begin{equation*}
  N=\max_y\,\ln\frac{\chi}{\chi_\infty}
\end{equation*}
does not depend on $\chi_\infty$. The table gives the growth rate
$\Delta N/\Delta Re_\theta$ for a range of $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$, where
$\Delta Re_\theta$ is the increase of $Re_\theta$ from $N=1$ to $N=5$ and
$\Delta N=4$, as a fraction of the Drela--Giles slope
$dN/dRe_\theta=1.039\times10^{-2}$.

The growth rate is nearly independent of the onset factor $\con$. The table is

Drela--Giles slope, but it sharpens the wall-normal profile of $\tilde\nu$,
which the grid must resolve. The wall-normal length scale of that profile is proportional to
$\sqrt{c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}}$, by dimensional analysis: the diffusion
coefficient $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\nu/\sigma$ and the growth rate
$a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:lamdiff} form only one length,
$\sqrt{c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}\,\nu/(\sigma\,a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega)}$. The model uses
\begin{equation*}
  c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}=\tfrac16 .
\end{equation*}

The factor $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$ leaves SA's solution of a turbulent wall layer
unchanged. That solution, $\tilde\nu=\kappa u_\tau d$ with $u_\tau$ the friction velocity, is linear in the wall
distance, and the molecular diffusion of a linear profile is zero whatever its
coefficient. The factor is also absent from the free shear flows on which
Spalart and Allmaras~\cite{spalart_allmaras_1994} calibrated $c_{b1}$,
$\sigma$ and $c_{b2}$: their equation for those flows does not contain the
molecular viscosity. The flat-plate boundary layer at $Re_\theta=10^4$, on
which they calibrated $c_{w2}$, does change, because $\chi$ decreases through
one at its outer edge. With $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}=\tfrac16$ in SA, its
skin-friction coefficient is $0.07\%$ higher, and its eddy viscosity differs by
at most $0.25\%$ of its peak.

Diffusion also delays the growth, and the onset factor $\con$ compensates for
the delay. It lowers the whole threshold of Eq.~\eqref{w:onset}, so the growth
starts farther upstream. On the Drela--Giles envelope $N=1$ at
$Re_\theta=338$. With $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}=\tfrac16$ and $\con=1$ the marched
Blasius boundary layer reaches $N=1$ at $Re_\theta=398$, $18\%$ late. With
$\con=\tfrac12$ it reaches $N=1$ at $Re_\theta=281$. The model uses
\begin{equation*}
  \con=0.712 ,
\end{equation*}
with which $N=1$ at $Re_\theta=331$, $2\%$ before the Drela--Giles station.
The march uses $3200$ streamwise steps and $9600$ wall-normal cells. Halving
either spacing again lowers the $N=1$ stations by less than $1\%$ and changes
the growth rates by less than $0.2\%$. Halving the width $w$ of the onset gate,
Eq.~\eqref{w:rate}, moves the $N=1$ stations of the attached Falkner--Skan
family by at most $1.6\%$, and doubling it by at most $3.3\%$.

\subsection{Handover of the Production to SA}

\laozi[7.6pt]{9}

Equation~\eqref{w:lamdiff} describes $\tilde\nu$ while $\chi\ll1$, where the eddy
viscosity is negligible. Above $\chi\approx1$ the eddy viscosity affects the
mean flow significantly, and the equation of $\tilde\nu$ must become SA's. Near $\chi=1$ the
production must therefore change from the amplification source to SA's own. In Eq.~\eqref{w:blend} the handover weight $\sigma_P$ of
Eq.~\eqref{w:sigma} multiplies SA's production, and $1-\sigma_P$ multiplies
the amplification source. The weight $\sigma_P$ is zero for $\chi\le1$ and
rises toward one over the width $\tau$. With $\tau=4$, $\sigma_P=0.78$ at
$\chi=c_{v1}=7.1$ and $\sigma_P=0.96$ at $\chi=2c_{v1}=14.2$.

The production of Eq.~\eqref{w:blend} is the larger of the two weighted
sources. Below $\chi=1$ it is the amplification source. Above $\chi=1$ it
remains the amplification source, multiplied by $1-\sigma_P$, up to the value
of $\chi$ at which SA's weighted production exceeds it. Beyond that value the
production is SA's alone, multiplied by $\sigma_P$, and the amplification
source does not affect the equation. The maximum, in place of the sum of the
two weighted sources, is an empirical choice. With the sum, many of the
airfoil cases of Section~\ref{sec:results} do not converge when the
$\tilde\nu$ equation and the mean-flow equations are solved in turn, without a
joint Jacobian. A feedback loop is a possible cause. The displacement
thickness changes rapidly across transition. That change alters the pressure
gradient upstream, and with it the shape of the boundary-layer profile there.
The amplification source is a function of that shape. With the sum it remains
in the equation wherever $\sigma_P<1$, and so the altered shape changes
transition in turn. With the maximum it does not.

Like the factor $c_{\nu,\mathrm{ai}}$ of Section~II.D, the handover weights
must leave SA's solution of a turbulent wall layer unchanged. SA's solution in
the constant-stress wall layer is the linear profile
$\tilde\nu=\kappa u_\tau d$, so $\chi=\kappa y^+$. Every value of $\sigma_P$
between zero and one therefore occurs in that layer: $\sigma_P$ is zero for
$y^+<2.4$, $0.78$ at $y^+=17$ and $0.96$ at $y^+=35$. On that profile SA's
production, destruction and diffusion are $c_{b1}u_\tau^2$,
$c_{w1}\kappa^2u_\tau^2$ and $(1+c_{b2})\kappa^2u_\tau^2/\sigma$. Each is
independent of the wall distance, and they balance by the definition of
$c_{w1}$. Multiplying the production by $\sigma_P$ breaks the balance wherever
$\sigma_P<1$, unless the destruction changes with it.
Requiring the balance for every value of $\sigma_P$,
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_P\,c_{b1}-\sigma_D\,c_{w1}\kappa^2+\frac{(1+c_{b2})\,\kappa^2}{\sigma}=0,
\end{equation*}
gives the destruction weight of Eq.~\eqref{w:sigma}. The weight $\sigma_D$ is a
linear function of $\sigma_P$, with slope $c_{b1}/(\kappa^2c_{w1})\approx0.249$
and value $(1+c_{b2})/(\sigma c_{w1})\approx0.751$ at $\sigma_P=0$.

With this tie the linear profile remains a solution of the full equation, for
any $\tau$, to within $8\times10^{-6}$ of SA's production. The only term left
over is the amplification source, which is the whole production where
$\sigma_P=0$, that is for $y^+<2.4$. It is that small because there the onset
gate $\sigma_{Re}$ is $0.003$ and $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is at most $0.002$.
Wherever $\sigma_P$ exceeds about $10^{-5}$, SA's weighted production is the
larger of the two, and the balance holds to rounding error.

In the laminar regime $\sigma_P=0$ and $\sigma_D=0.751$, so three quarters of
SA's destruction remains. It does not change Eq.~\eqref{w:lamdiff}: SA's
destruction is of second order in $\tilde\nu$, and Eq.~\eqref{w:lamdiff}
contains the terms of first order. The destruction is
below $10^{-3}$ of the amplification production up to the $\chi=1$ crossing,
and at most $1.4\%$ of the $\sigma_P$-weighted SA production at $\chi=c_{v1}$.

\subsection{Eddy Viscosity without Wall Damping in Separated Shear Layers}

\laozi[7.6pt]{41}

The transport equation of $\tilde\nu$ is complete with the handover of
Section~II.E. The mean flow depends on $\tilde\nu$ only through the eddy
viscosity $\mu_t$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:ns}. SA's eddy viscosity is
$\rho\tilde\nu f_{v1}(\chi)$, and the model alters it in one situation: a
shear layer that has separated from the wall. The reason is an observation.
With SA's eddy viscosity unaltered, the laminar separation bubbles computed
with the model reattach too late, or not at all. With the alteration they
reattach earlier, although still later than measured (Sections~III.C
and~III.D).

SA's damping function $f_{v1}$ is designed for the wall layer: the inner
layer of a fully developed turbulent boundary layer, where the velocity
obeys the law of the wall.
Spalart and Allmaras~\cite{spalart_allmaras_1994} introduce it for the
near-wall region only: in their calibration on free shear flows the
transported quantity is the eddy viscosity itself, without damping. SA's
solution in the wall layer, $\tilde\nu=\kappa u_\tau d$, is linear all the way to the wall,
while the eddy viscosity of a turbulent wall layer decreases toward the wall
as a higher power of the wall distance. Multiplying by $f_{v1}$ produces that
decrease: $\tilde\nu f_{v1}$ is proportional to $d^4$ where $\chi\ll c_{v1}$.
The function $f_{v1}$ can be a function of $\chi$ alone because
$\chi=\kappa y^+$ in that layer: $f_{v1}$ is one half at $\chi=c_{v1}$, which
is $y^+\approx17$.

A separated shear layer passes through the same values of $\chi$ with no wall
layer present. Downstream of a laminar separation the disturbance grows in a
shear layer that has lifted off the wall, and $\chi$ increases through one
inside it. The damping then removes most of the eddy viscosity while $\chi$ is
of the order of $c_{v1}$: $f_{v1}=0.07$ at $\chi=3$. That eddy viscosity is
what reattaches the flow.

The model must therefore distinguish a wall layer from a lifted
shear layer, with local quantities. The law of the wall provides the test. In
a wall layer $\chi$ determines the wall coordinate,
$y^+=\chi/\kappa$, and the law of the wall then determines the velocity,
$U^+(y^+)$. Their product, $y^+U^+$, is the Reynolds number $|\mathbf u|\,d/\nu$ of a
wall-layer point with that $\chi$. The indicator $q$ is the ratio of the
actual $|\mathbf u|\,d/\nu$ to that product,
\begin{equation}
  q = \frac{|\mathbf u|\,d/\nu}{\,(\chi/\kappa)\;U^+(\chi/\kappa)\,},
  \qquad
  U^+(y^+)=\tfrac1\kappa\ln(1+\kappa y^+)
  +7.8\big(1-e^{-y^+/11}-\tfrac{y^+}{11}e^{-y^+/3}\big),
  \label{w:fv1q}
\end{equation}
with Reichardt's formula~\cite{reichardt_1951} for $U^+$. In the wall layer
$q$ is close to one. With the velocity of SA's own solution in that layer,
$q$ is between $0.96$ and $1.07$ at every $\chi$. In a lifted shear layer $q$ is well above one, because the point
is farther from the wall and moves faster than a point of a wall layer with
the same $\chi$.

The bypass weight $\sigma_{v1}$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:ns} is the product of two
gates,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{v1} = \sigma_\chi\,\sigma_q, \qquad
  \sigma_\chi=\operatorname{clip}\!\big\langle\chi-1\big\rangle_0^1, \qquad
  \sigma_q=\operatorname{clip}\!\big\langle(q-2)/2\big\rangle_0^1 .
  \label{w:fv1bypass}
\end{equation}
Where $\sigma_{v1}=1$ the eddy viscosity is $\rho\tilde\nu$, without damping.
The gate $\sigma_\chi$ is zero for $\chi\le1$ and one for $\chi\ge2$. It keeps
the eddy viscosity out of the laminar regime, where $q$ is far above four.
Without it the eddy viscosity at $\chi=0.5$ would be half the molecular
viscosity. The range $\chi<1$ would then not be ``empty'': $\tilde\nu$ would
affect the mean flow there. Above $\chi=30$, $f_{v1}>0.98$, making the eddy viscosity in
Eq.~\eqref{w:ns} insensitive to $\sigma_{v1}$, so $\sigma_{v1}$ affects it only for $\chi$ between $1$ and
$30$.

The gate $\sigma_q$ opens between $q=2$ and $q=4$. It starts at two because the
outer part of an attached turbulent boundary layer is faster than the law of
the wall. On Coles' composite profile~\cite{coles_1956} this excess increases
with the adverse pressure gradient and decreases with the Reynolds number. In
zero pressure gradient at $Re_\theta\approx780$ ($H=1.54$), the largest $q$
for $\chi$ between $1$ and $30$ is $1.02$. In an adverse pressure gradient
about $7$ times the wall shear stress divided by the displacement
thickness~\cite{das_1987}, it is $1.15$ at $Re_\theta\approx1700$ ($H=1.95$)
and $1.88$ at $Re_\theta\approx520$ ($H=2.27$).

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Form of the Indicators}

In three dimensions the triple $(X,Y,Z)$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:ind} is formed from
four local vectors: the
velocity $\mathbf u$, the vorticity $\boldsymbol\omega$, the wall-distance
vector $\mathbf d=d\,\nabla d$, and the curvature vector
$\boldsymbol\ell=\tfrac12\langle\mathbf d,\mathbf d\rangle\,\nabla^2\mathbf u$,
with $\nabla^2\mathbf u=-\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega$ giving the second
derivative:
\begin{equation}
  (X,\,Y,\,Z) \;=\;
  \big(\,|\mathbf u|,\;\; d\,|\boldsymbol\omega|,\;\;
  \langle\boldsymbol\ell,\hat{\mathbf u}\rangle\,\big),
  \qquad \hat{\mathbf u}=\mathbf u/|\mathbf u| .
  \label{w:ratio}
\end{equation}
Each term has the dimension of velocity. In a thin two-dimensional boundary
layer with positive $u$ and $u'$, the three reduce to the terms of
Eq.~\eqref{w:ind}. The solver assembles the same triple multiplied by
$|\mathbf u|$,
\begin{equation}
  |\mathbf u|\,(X,\,Y,\,Z) \;=\;
  \Big(\,\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle,\;
  \sqrt{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle\,
        \langle\mathbf d,\mathbf d\rangle\,
        \langle\boldsymbol\omega,\boldsymbol\omega\rangle},\;
  \langle\boldsymbol\ell,\mathbf u\rangle\,\Big),
  \label{w:gram}
\end{equation}
which requires no division by $|\mathbf u|$. The common factor leaves
$\hat\Omega$ and $\hat I$ unchanged, because both depend only on ratios of the
three terms. Every term of Eqs.~\eqref{w:ratio} and~\eqref{w:gram} is a
function of inner products of vectors alone. Both forms are therefore
invariant under any rotation or reflection: a rotated or mirrored flow gives
the same $(X,Y,Z)$ at corresponding points, in any Cartesian coordinate
system.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\laozi[7.6pt]{81}

Each case in this section is chosen for one thing that it shows about the
model. The flat plate shows the role of the freestream value $\chi_\infty$,
which is the model's $N_\mathrm{crit}$. The NLF(1)-0416 airfoil shows
transition in an attached boundary layer, at a location that changes with the
pressure gradient. The Eppler 387 airfoil shows transition in a laminar
separation bubble. The same airfoil at lower Reynolds numbers shows the
condition at which the computed bubble no longer reattaches while the measured
bubble does. A two-element section shows a boundary layer that receives
$\tilde\nu$ from the wake of another element. The circular cylinder shows
transition moving from the separated wake onto the wall as the Reynolds number increases
through ten decades. The Daedalus wing shows the separation bubble on a wing whose chord Reynolds
number changes along the span.
The 6:1 spheroid is a three-dimensional body on which crossflow instability,
which the model does not represent, causes transition at the higher
incidences.

The results are computed with Flexcompute Flow (formerly known as Flow360), an unstructured, node-centered,
second-order finite-volume solver with the Roe flux, at $M=0.1$ unless stated
otherwise. The airfoil
cases use the freestream value $\chi_\infty=c_{v1}e^{-9}\approx8.76\times10^{-4}$,
which is $N_\mathrm{crit}=9$ and, by Mack's correlation, $Tu\approx0.07\%$
(Eq.~\eqref{w:mack} in Section~III.A).

The airfoil cases are computed on two independently
generated grid families. The unstructured grids are generated with
cavity-based operations~\cite{loseille_alauzet_menier_2017} and consist of
triangles everywhere, including in the boundary layers. The structured grids
are all-quadrilateral O-grids. Each family is refined at three levels, from
$1.5\times10^4$ to $2.6\times10^5$ nodes. The levels are called L0, L1 and L2,
from the coarsest to the finest, and every length scale of the grid is halved
from one level to the next.

A transition location is
the station where the largest $\chi$ near the wall crosses one. On the L2
grids the $\chi=c_{v1}$ crossing, where there is one, is $0.002$ to
$0.07\,c$ farther aft on the
NLF(1)-0416, with a median of $0.02\,c$, and $0.004$ to $0.30\,c$ farther aft
on the Eppler 387, with a median of $0.07\,c$. Bubble stations are
the zero crossings of the signed $C_f$.

The $e^9$ reference in the airfoil
figures is a coupled viscous--inviscid panel computation with the $e^N$ method
at $N_\mathrm{crit}=9$. It is computed with mfoil~\cite{fidkowski_mfoil_2022},
or with XFOIL~\cite{drela_xfoil_1989} or Flexcompute
Foil~\cite{smith_flexfoil_2026}, a reimplementation of XFOIL in Rust, where
mfoil does not converge. The model is also implemented in a second solver,
OpenFOAM v2412, the OpenCFD release. This replication is computed with simpleFoam, a cell-centered,
pressure-based solver for steady incompressible flow, on a flat-plate
grid of the same dimensions and on the same structured airfoil grids. It omits the $f_{v1}$ bypass of Section~II.F. It covers thirty
airfoil cases and the flat-plate sweep.

Every computation is converged with a reduced CFL number for the
$\tilde\nu$ equation in the laminar regime, so that the mean flow settles
before $\tilde\nu$ responds to the startup transient. The pseudo-time step of
the $\tilde\nu$ equation is multiplied by $10^{-2}$ where $\chi\le c_{v1}$.
Above $c_{v1}$ the factor is $10^{-2\exp[-(\chi-c_{v1})/4]}$, which
increases to one.

\subsection{The Blasius Flat Plate}

The flat plate is the case in which the freestream turbulence intensity is
varied. The freestream value $\chi_\infty$ is the input that determines how far
the disturbance must grow, as $N_\mathrm{crit}$ is in the $e^N$ method. The two are
related by
\begin{equation}
  \chi_\infty = c_{v1}\,e^{-N_\mathrm{crit}},
  \qquad
  N_\mathrm{crit}(Tu) = -8.43 - 2.4\,\ln Tu ,
  \label{w:mack}
\end{equation}
where $N_\mathrm{crit}(Tu)$ is Mack's correlation~\cite{mack_1977}, with the
turbulence intensity $Tu$ as a fraction. With this definition the amplification
factor $N=\max_y\ln(\chi/\chi_\infty)$ of Section~II.D equals
$N_\mathrm{crit}$ where the largest $\chi$ in the boundary layer is
$c_{v1}=7.1$. It is $N_\mathrm{crit}-2.0$ where the largest $\chi$ is one. A
change of $\chi_\infty$ by the factor $e$ changes $N_\mathrm{crit}$ by one. On
the Blasius envelope, where $dN/dRe_\theta\approx10^{-2}$, that moves the
onset by $\Delta Re_\theta\approx100$. At $Tu=1\%$ the correlation gives
$N_\mathrm{crit}\approx2.6$, and at $Tu\approx3\%$ it gives $\chi_\infty=c_{v1}$.
These are conditions of bypass transition, which the model does not represent.

The plate is computed at five turbulence intensities from $Tu=0.04\%$ to
$0.6\%$, for which Eq.~\eqref{w:mack} gives $\chi_\infty\approx2.3\times10^{-4}$,
$1.2\times10^{-3}$, $6.3\times10^{-3}$, $2.9\times10^{-2}$ and
$1.5\times10^{-1}$. The grid is structured, $320\times80$, with
$y^+\lesssim0.3$, and extends to $Re_x=6\times10^6$.

Figure~\ref{f:flatplate} shows the five computations. Along the plate $\chi$
grows nearly exponentially from $\chi_\infty$, while the skin friction remains
that of the Blasius boundary layer. Where the largest $\chi$ crosses one the
skin friction begins to rise. It reaches its turbulent level shortly after the
largest $\chi$ crosses $c_{v1}$. A higher turbulence intensity gives a larger
$\chi_\infty$, and both crossings occur at a lower $Re_x$.

\subsection{The Eppler 387 Airfoil}

The Eppler 387 at $Re=2\times10^5$ is the standard case for transition in a
laminar separation bubble. The laminar boundary layer on the upper surface
separates before $\chi$ reaches one. The inflection point of the separated
shear layer's profile is far from the wall, so $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is close to
one and $\chi$ grows at nearly the largest rate, $a_{\max}\omega$. The eddy
viscosity that follows returns the flow to the wall as a turbulent boundary
layer. The region of reversed flow between separation and reattachment is the
bubble. McGhee, Walker and Millard~\cite{mcghee_1988} measured the forces of
this airfoil in the LTPT, and the separation and reattachment stations by oil
flow. The Eppler 387 is also the airfoil on which Drela demonstrated XFOIL at
low Reynolds numbers~\cite{drela_xfoil_1989}, against Langley measurements by
McGhee and coworkers.

The computations cover incidences from $\alpha=-4^\circ$ to $9^\circ$.
Figure~\ref{f:eppcf5} shows the quantities of the model along the chord at
$\alpha=5^\circ$ and $6^\circ$. On the upper surface $\hat\Omega\hat I$
increases from about $0.1$ in the attached boundary layer to nearly one over
the bubble. The largest $\chi$ grows from $\chi_\infty$ and crosses one
inside the bubble. The pressure is nearly constant over the forward part of
the bubble and rises steeply after $\chi$ exceeds $c_{v1}$. The skin friction
is negative between separation and reattachment. The gray band is the bubble
measured by oil flow. On the lower surface $\hat\Omega\hat I$ is below $0.06$,
and $Re_\Omega$ remains below the onset threshold of Eq.~\eqref{w:onset}. The
rate $a\,\sigma_{Re}\,\omega$ is therefore small, and $\chi$ remains near
$\chi_\infty$ over most of the chord.

\subsection{Reynolds-Number Study of the Eppler 387 Airfoil}

The length of the bubble increases as the Reynolds number decreases. Below
some Reynolds number the separated shear layer does not reattach ahead of the
trailing edge, the lift decreases and the drag increases severalfold. This is
the bursting of the bubble. McGhee's measurements cover $Re=6\times10^4$ to
$4.6\times10^5$. This study shows the Reynolds number at which the computed
bubble bursts, and compares it with the measured one.

The airfoil is computed at $\alpha=5^\circ$ and $Re=6\times10^4$, $10^5$,
$3\times10^5$ and $4.6\times10^5$, on the six grids of Section~III.C, with the
same $\chi_\infty$. Figure~\ref{f:eppresweepforces} shows the lift, drag,
moment and bubble stations against the Reynolds number, with the results at
$2\times10^5$ from Section~III.C. Table~\ref{t:eppresweep} lists the
coefficients on the L2 grids. At $2\times10^5$ and above, the three grid
levels converge toward the measurement, and the computed bubble shortens as
the Reynolds number increases. The L2 drag is $0.0155$ and $0.0148$ against a
measured $0.0138$ at $2\times10^5$, and $0.0087$ and $0.0089$ against
$0.0093$ at $4.6\times10^5$.

At $Re=10^5$ the measured flow reattaches at $0.67\,c$. The computed skin
friction returns to zero only within $0.05\,c$ of the trailing edge
(Fig.~\ref{f:eppresweeplow}). The L2 drag is $0.0410$ on the structured grid
and $0.0307$ on the unstructured grid, $73\%$ and $30\%$ above the measured
$0.0237$. Refinement increases the difference. At $Re=6\times10^4$ the
computed flow does not reattach. The measured flow at this Reynolds number is
found both with and without reattachment at the same
incidence~\cite{mcghee_1988}. The computed bubble therefore bursts
between $Re=10^5$ and $2\times10^5$ at this incidence. The measured bubble
reattaches at $10^5$ and is at its limit at $6\times10^4$.

\subsection{A Two-Element Section}

In every case so far, a single boundary layer develops on each surface, and
$\chi$ in it grows from the freestream value. On a multi-element section the wake of one
element passes over the next element. This case shows what the model computes
then. The section was designed for the purpose, and there is no measurement
of it. Its main element is laminar on both surfaces over a narrow range of
incidence. A laminar separation bubble forms on the upper surface of its
flap, a NACA~9416. Changing the incidence changes which surface of the main element is
turbulent, and with it the $\chi$ of the fluid that passes over the flap.
Table~\ref{t:twoelementgeom} defines the geometry. The Reynolds number is
$10^6$ on the total chord, and $\chi_\infty=c_{v1}e^{-9}$. The incidence is
swept from $\alpha=-9^\circ$ to $+13^\circ$ on the L0 and L1 grids and to
$+11^\circ$ on the L2 grid. The solutions are converged to a momentum residual
of $10^{-9}$, except the L2 solution at $-9^\circ$.

\subsection{The Cylinder Drag Crisis}

Near $Re_D=3\times10^5$ the drag coefficient of a circular cylinder decreases
to a fraction of its value: the drag crisis. Below the crisis the boundary
layer separates while it is laminar, ahead of the widest point of the
cylinder, and the wake is wide. Above it the boundary layer is turbulent
before its final separation, which is farther aft, and the wake is narrow.
The case shows the model moving transition from the separated shear layer
onto the wall as the Reynolds number increases, with the constants and the
equations of the airfoil cases.

The computations are steady and quasi-two-dimensional. They cover twenty-five
Reynolds numbers from $Re_D=1$ to $10^{10}$ with one freestream value,
$\chi_\infty=1.1\times10^{-2}$, which is $Tu=0.2\%$ in Eq.~\eqref{w:mack}.
Each computation starts from the converged solution at the next lower
Reynolds number. Four O-grids with $y^+\lesssim1$ cover the range. They are
identified by the thickness of their first layer, $y_1/R$: $2\times10^{-3}$
($600\times121$ points) for $Re_D\le10^3$, $1.6\times10^{-5}$
($1200\times148$) up to $1.78\times10^6$, $2\times10^{-6}$ ($1600\times170$)
up to $10^7$, and $2\times10^{-9}$ ($1600\times170$) above. At a change of
grid the solution is interpolated onto the new grid. A computation is
accepted as steady when $C_d$ changes by less than $10^{-3}$ over $1000$
iterations. Five of the twenty-five do not meet this criterion, and
Table~\ref{t:dragcrisis} gives the range of their $C_d$ in brackets.

Every solution is symmetric, with $|C_l|<2\times10^{-6}$. A steady symmetric
solution contains no vortex shedding. Between the onset of shedding at
$Re_D\approx46$ and the crisis, the computed drag is lower than the measured
drag: the computed $C_d$ is $0.84$ between $Re_D=10^4$ and $10^5$, and the
measured $C_d$ is between $1.0$ and $1.2$ (Fig.~\ref{f:dragcrisiscd}). Below
the onset of shedding the steady solution is the physical flow. The computed
$C_d$ is $10.67$ at $Re_D=1$ and $2.797$ at $Re_D=10$, which is $1.7\%$ below
the value of Dennis and Chang~\cite{dennis_chang_1970}. The largest $\chi$
remains near $\chi_\infty$ up to $Re_D=30$ (Fig.~\ref{f:dragcrisisangles}).

Reynolds number, the angle of the first separation and the transition angle
$\theta_{tr}$, both measured from the forward stagnation point. The
transition angle is the smallest angle at which $\chi$ reaches one on a
radial ray from the wall. From $Re_D=10^5$ to $3\times10^5$ the boundary layer

\subsection{The Daedalus Wing}

The wing of the Daedalus human-powered aircraft~\cite{drela_daedalus_1988} is
the first three-dimensional case. A laminar separation bubble forms on the upper
surface of each section of the wing, as on the Eppler 387. The wing adds the
variation along the span: the chord Reynolds number decreases over the
tapered outer part, and the flow at the tip is three-dimensional.

The half-span is $17.07\,$m, the root chord $0.917\,$m, and the aspect ratio
$37.8$. The chord is constant to the spanwise station $\eta=0.88$ and tapers
from there to the tip. The sections are the DAE-11, DAE-21 and DAE-31
airfoils. The root-chord Reynolds number is $5\times10^5$, and the incidences
are $\alpha=4^\circ$, $5^\circ$ and $6^\circ$, for which
$C_L\approx1.02$--$1.23$. The freestream value is
$\chi_\infty=8.76\times10^{-6}$, which is $N_\mathrm{crit}\approx13.6$, a
value representative of flight in still air. There are two grid families, each at three
levels: a structured O-grid stacked along the span, with $0.72$ to $36.7$
million nodes, and an unstructured grid of prisms, tetrahedra and hexahedra,
with $1.70$ to $54.9$ million nodes. There is no measurement of the
transition on this wing. The reference is the vortex-lattice code
AVL~\cite{drela_youngren_avl} with section polars from
XFOIL~\cite{drela_xfoil_1989} at the local lift coefficient and the same
$N_\mathrm{crit}$, at the total lift of the present computation.\footnote{The distributions of the reference along the chord in
Figs.~\ref{f:daesurf}, \ref{f:daesurfall} and~\ref{f:daesections}, and its section drag in
Fig.~\ref{f:daepolar}b, are computed with Flexcompute Foil~\cite{smith_flexfoil_2026}
at the same section lift, Reynolds number and $N_\mathrm{crit}$.}

Figure~\ref{f:daesurf} shows the upper surface at $\alpha=4^\circ$: the
pressure, the streamwise skin friction, and the largest $\chi$ near the wall.
The upper two rows are the L2 grids of the two grid families, and the bottom

last $0.8\%$ of the span, $\eta\gtrsim0.992$, the largest $\chi$ exceeds one
from the leading edge. The tip vortex is there, and the two-dimensional
reference contains no such region.

\subsection{The 6:1 Spheroid}

The 6:1 prolate spheroid at incidence is the standard three-dimensional case
for transition models. Kreplin, Vollmers and Meier~\cite{kreplin_1985}
measured the wall shear stress over the body with surface hot films in the
DFVLR tunnel at G\"ottingen, and there is one more measurement from the ONERA
F1 tunnel. The stability analysis of Stock~\cite{stock_2006} identifies the
cause of transition at each condition. At $Re_L=1.5\times10^6$ the boundary
layer is laminar up to its separation, and the measured line is the
separation line. At $Re_L\approx7\times10^6$ and zero or small incidence, the
TS waves cause transition. At $Re_L\gtrsim6.5\times10^6$ and
$\alpha\ge5^\circ$, the crossflow instability causes it, alone or together
with the TS waves. The model represents the TS waves only. The case shows
what it computes in each of the three situations.

The geometry is the analytic spheroid of length $L$ and largest diameter
$L/6$, as a half-model on a structured O-grid of $2.2$ million nodes with
a first wall spacing of $1.5\times10^{-6}L$. The constants of the model are
those of every other case. The Mach number is that of the tunnel. The
G\"ottingen tunnel is atmospheric, so its Mach number is proportional to
$Re_L$: $M=1.994\times10^{-8}\,Re_L$, which is $0.030$ at
$Re_L=1.52\times10^6$ and $0.144$ at $7.2\times10^6$. This relation is fitted
to the two published pairs of $Re_L$ and velocity, $7.70\times10^6$ at
$55.0$~m/s and $6.54\times10^6$ at $45.0$~m/s, and reproduces them within
$2\%$. The ONERA F1 tunnel is pressurized, and its condition is computed at
the Mach number of the G\"ottingen condition with the same $Re_L$. Each
computation is iterated $45\,000$ times from a uniform initial condition.

Two values of the turbulence intensity are available for each tunnel, and
they differ by a factor of up to three. Each condition is therefore computed
with two values of $\chi_\infty$. The fitted value is the one that
Eq.~\eqref{w:mack} gives for the $N_\mathrm{crit}$ that Stock fitted to the
measured transition: $8.0$ for G\"ottingen and $7.0$ for ONERA F1. The
measured value is the one for the turbulence intensity measured with a hot
wire: $0.33\%$ for G\"ottingen and less than $0.1\%$ for
ONERA F1. Table~\ref{tab:sphtunnel} lists the computed transition location minus the
measured one, in units of $L$, at every azimuth with a measurement.

At $Re_L=1.5\times10^6$ the computed transition location is within $0.03\,L$ to
$0.10\,L$ of the measured separation line, in the root mean square over the
measured azimuths. Figure~\ref{f:ov_a10hi}a shows $\alpha=10^\circ$ with the
measured turbulence intensity, where the root-mean-square difference is
$0.027\,L$ over all eight azimuths. With the fitted $\chi_\infty$ it is
$0.082\,L$. At $\alpha=5^\circ$ it is $0.068\,L$ with the measured turbulence
intensity, where the largest $\chi$ reaches one ahead of $x/L=0.97$ at seven
of the eight azimuths, and $0.028\,L$ with the fitted $\chi_\infty$, at four
of the eight. At $\alpha=29.7^\circ$ and $Re_L=1.53\times10^6$ it is
$0.098\,L$ and $0.095\,L$, over twelve azimuths.

\section{Remarks on the Operating Envelope}\label{sec:envelope}

\laozi[7.6pt]{1}

Section~III computes the model in boundary layers on fixed walls of small
curvature. This section evaluates it in five situations outside that range: a
rotating frame, a curved wall, a swept attachment line, a wall with suction,
and the wake of another surface. No flow is computed here. The first four are
exact solutions, or exact perturbations of the Blasius profile, on which the
three terms of Eq.~\eqref{w:ind} can be written down. The fifth is discussed
with the computation of Section~III.E. Each remark states what stability
theory or experiment finds in the flow, and what the model computes.
Figure~\ref{f:envsphere} shows three of the flows on the
indicator sphere of Fig.~\ref{f:sphere}.

\subsection{Rotation}

Consider a fluid in solid-body rotation together with its container, at the
angular velocity $\Omega_\mathrm{f}$. In the frame of the container the fluid
is at rest. It contains no shear, and it is
stable~\cite{drazin_reid_1981}. In an inertial frame the vorticity of the same
fluid is $2\Omega_\mathrm{f}$, and at the distance $d$ from the wall it moves
at the speed $\Omega_\mathrm{f}d$ relative to the nearest point of the wall.
With these two quantities the triple of Eq.~\eqref{w:ratio} is
$(X,Y,Z)=(\Omega_\mathrm{f}d,\;2\Omega_\mathrm{f}d,\;0)$. Its direction is
$(1,2,0)/\sqrt5$ at every point of the fluid, the large dot in
Fig.~\ref{f:envsphere}. There $\hat\Omega=2/\sqrt5$, $\hat I=1/\sqrt5$ and
$\hat\Omega\hat I=2/5$. The rate of Eq.~\eqref{w:a} is $0.4\,a_{\max}$, and
the gate of Eq.~\eqref{w:rate} is open where
$Re_\Omega=2\Omega_\mathrm{f}d^2/\nu$ exceeds the threshold of
Eq.~\eqref{w:onset}, which is $133$ at this value of $\hat\Omega\hat I$. The
model therefore amplifies $\tilde\nu$ in a fluid that is at rest relative to
its walls.

A boundary layer in a frame that rotates about a spanwise axis is found on
the blade of a vertical-axis turbine and in the passage of a radial impeller.
Stability theory finds two effects of the rotation. A two-dimensional TS wave
is unaffected by it~\cite{schmid_henningson_2001}. Streamwise roll cells,
driven by the Coriolis force, grow where the frame rotates in the sense
opposite to the vorticity of the boundary layer. Where the frame rotates in
the sense of that vorticity the flow is stabilized: in a rotating channel the
transition to turbulence is suppressed on that side~\cite{johnston_1972}.

With the absolute vorticity the model's rate depends on the rotation. It
increases where the rotation stabilizes the flow, and it decreases where the
roll cells grow. On the Blasius profile the largest $\hat\Omega\hat I$ changes
by the fraction $18.3\,(\delta_\nu/L)(\Omega_\mathrm{f}L/U_\infty)$, with
$\delta_\nu=\sqrt{\nu x/U_e}$. For $\Omega_\mathrm{f}=U_\infty/L$, with $L$ the chord or the diameter,
that is $0.6\%$ at the transition location of the NLF(1)-0416 at
$\alpha=0^\circ$, $2.9\%$ on the Eppler 387 at $\alpha=0^\circ$, and $4.9\%$
on the cylinder at $Re_D=10^5$. It is larger at a lower Reynolds number,
because $\delta_\nu/L$ is proportional to $(U_\infty L/\nu)^{-1/2}$. With the vorticity and the velocity relative to the
rotating frame, the model is independent of the rotation, as the TS waves
are, and it represents no roll cells.

\subsection{Wall Curvature}

The curvature of the wall changes the terms of Eq.~\eqref{w:ind} by a small
fraction. That fraction matters because $\hat I$ is a difference: $Y-X-Z$ can
be small compared with the three terms. At the wall distance of the largest
$\hat\Omega\hat I$, the ratio $|Y-X-Z|/(|X|+|Y|+|Z|)$ is $0.04$ on the
Falkner--Skan profile $\beta=+0.10$, $0.08$ on the Blasius profile, $0.16$ at
$\beta=-0.10$ and $0.39$ at incipient separation. A given small change of one
term is therefore a larger fraction of $\hat I$ in a favorable pressure
gradient than near separation.

A wall of radius $R$ that curves in the streamwise direction turns the flow
with it. On a convex wall the centrifugal force is
stabilizing~\cite{drazin_reid_1981}. The model contains the curvature in the
Laplacian of the velocity. For a velocity $u(r)$ along circles about the
center of curvature,
$\nabla^2\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=u''+u'/r-u/r^2$, with the derivatives
taken along $r$, and $r=R+d$ on a convex wall. To first order in $d/R$ the
curvature term of Eq.~\eqref{w:ratio} increases by $(d/2R)\,Y$, and $\hat I$
decreases. On the Blasius profile the largest $\hat\Omega\hat I$ changes by
the fraction $-4.8\,\delta_\nu/R$. The rate of the model is therefore lower on
a convex wall, the direction of the stabilizing effect. This change is part
of every computation of Section~III. On the upper surfaces at
$\alpha=0^\circ$, which are convex, it is $-0.15\%$ at the transition
location of the NLF(1)-0416 and $-0.32\%$ at the separation of the
Eppler 387.

On a concave wall the centrifugal force is destabilizing, and the boundary
layer forms streamwise vortices, the G\"ortler
vortices~\cite{saric_1994}. Their growth is a function of the G\"ortler
number $G_\theta=Re_\theta\sqrt{\theta/R}$. In the model $r=R-d$ on a
concave wall, the curvature term decreases by $(d/2R)\,Y$, and the largest
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ of the Blasius profile changes by the fraction
$+4.8\,\delta_\nu/R$. The rate of the model is therefore higher on a concave
wall, the direction of the destabilizing effect. The increase does not
contain the Reynolds number. At $\delta_\nu/R=10^{-3}$ it is $0.48\%$ at
every Reynolds number, while $G_\theta$ is $17$ at $Re_x=10^6$ and $171$ at
$Re_x=10^8$. The model represents no streamwise vortices.

On a slender body of revolution of radius $r_0$ in axial flow, the wall
curves across the stream. Stability theory finds this boundary layer more
stable than that of a flat plate: on a long thin cylinder it is stable below
a critical Reynolds number, and above it the flow is unstable only over a
finite distance~\cite{tutty_2002}. In the model, an axial velocity $u(r)$
with $r=r_0+d$ gives $\nabla^2\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=u''+u'/r$, and
the curvature term increases by $(d/2r)\,Y$. To first order this is the
change on a convex wall with $r_0$ in place of $R$, and the rate of the model
is lower. At the eight measured stations of the spheroid at
$\alpha=10^\circ$ and $Re_L=1.5\times10^6$, the largest $\hat\Omega\hat I$ of
a Blasius profile is lower by $3.0\%$ to $8.8\%$. On a body of fineness ratio
$40$ at the same $Re_L$ and stations it is lower by $18.3\%$ to $46.1\%$, and
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ is nowhere positive where $\delta_\nu$ equals $r_0$.

The term of transverse curvature depends on the form of the second
derivative. Equation~\eqref{w:ratio} uses $\nabla^2\mathbf u$. The wall-normal
derivative of the magnitude of the vorticity, which is the same quantity in a
plane boundary layer, contains the term of streamwise curvature and not that
of transverse curvature.

\subsection{Sweep}

On a swept wing the flow divides along the attachment line of the leading
edge. Outside the boundary layer the chordwise velocity increases linearly
with the distance $x$ from that line, $U_e=(dU_e/dx)\,x$, and the spanwise
velocity is $W_\infty$, the component of the freestream along the leading
edge. The swept Hiemenz flow is the exact solution of the Navier--Stokes
equations with this outer flow~\cite{hall_malik_poll_1984}. On the attachment
line its chordwise velocity is zero, and the boundary layer flows along the
line, in the spanwise direction. The thickness of that boundary layer is the
same at every spanwise station, because the flow toward the wall balances
the diffusion of vorticity away from it. The spanwise velocity is $W_\infty$
multiplied by a function of $d/\delta$ alone, with
$\delta=\sqrt{\nu/(dU_e/dx)}$, and the Reynolds number of the boundary layer
is $\bar R=W_\infty\delta/\nu$.

Linear theory finds waves that travel along the attachment line unstable
above $\bar R=583$~\cite{hall_malik_poll_1984}. With large disturbances,
turbulent flow along the attachment line is observed above
$\bar R\approx245$~\cite{poll_1979}. The model evaluates the spanwise
profile, the green curve in Fig.~\ref{f:envsphere}. Its largest
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ is $0.064$, which is $82\%$ of the $0.078$ of the Blasius
profile. The onset gate $\sigma_{Re}$ of Eq.~\eqref{w:rate} opens
gradually as $\bar R$ increases. The largest $a\,\sigma_{Re}$ across the
profile is $10\%$ of the largest $a$ at $\bar R=371$, one half at
$\bar R=603$ and $90\%$ at $\bar R=839$. The linear critical value, $583$, is
inside that range. At $\bar R=245$ the largest $a\,\sigma_{Re}$ is $3\%$ of
the largest $a$: the value $245$ is a property of large disturbances, and the
amplification of Section~II describes the growth of small ones.

Downstream of the attachment line the boundary layer of a swept wing contains
a crossflow. Its instability is not represented in the model, as
Section~III.H shows on the spheroid.

\subsection{Suction}

Uniform suction through the wall, at the velocity $v_w$, makes the boundary
layer thinner and lowers its shape factor $H$. Far downstream the profile is
the asymptotic suction profile, $u=U_\infty\big(1-e^{-d|v_w|/\nu}\big)$, an
exact solution of the Navier--Stokes equations. Its
shape factor is $2$, against $2.59$ for the Blasius profile. Its
displacement thickness is $\nu/|v_w|$, so $Re_{\delta^*}=U_\infty/|v_w|$. Its
critical Reynolds number is
$Re_{\delta^*}\approx5.4\times10^4$~\cite{hocking_1975}, about one hundred
times the $519$ of the Blasius profile~\cite{schmid_henningson_2001}. On this
profile the three terms of Eq.~\eqref{w:ind}, divided by $U_\infty$, and the
accumulated inflection that they form are
\begin{gather}
  X=1-e^{-d|v_w|/\nu}, \qquad
  Y=\frac{d\,|v_w|}{\nu}\,e^{-d|v_w|/\nu}, \qquad
  Z=-\frac12\Big(\frac{d\,|v_w|}{\nu}\Big)^{\!2}e^{-d|v_w|/\nu}, \notag\\[3pt]
  I = Y-X-Z = e^{-d|v_w|/\nu}\,\bigg[1+\frac{d\,|v_w|}{\nu}
      +\frac12\Big(\frac{d\,|v_w|}{\nu}\Big)^{\!2}\,\bigg]-1 .
  \label{w:suction}
\end{gather}
The polynomial in the square brackets of Eq.~\eqref{w:suction} consists of the
first three terms of the Taylor series of $e^{d|v_w|/\nu}$ in powers of
$d\,|v_w|/\nu$. It is smaller than $e^{d|v_w|/\nu}$ wherever $d>0$, so $I$ is
negative at every wall distance: the purple curve in
Fig.~\ref{f:envsphere} lies on the side of the sphere where
$\hat\Omega\hat I$ is negative. The rate of Eq.~\eqref{w:a} is zero at every
Reynolds number. The model therefore gives the direction of the effect of
suction and overstates its size: a boundary layer with
$|v_w|/U_\infty<1.8\times10^{-5}$ is unstable, and it remains laminar in the
model.

\subsection{The Wake of Another Surface}

The two-element section of Section~III.E is an example of the wake of an
upstream surface changing the transition location on a downstream surface.
In experiments the turbulence of such a wake causes the boundary layer of the
downstream surface to become turbulent farther upstream. In turbomachines
this wake-induced transition occurs on a blade row behind
another~\cite{mayle_1991}. In the model the wake acts through its
$\tilde\nu$. The destruction term of SA is proportional
to $(\tilde\nu/d)^2$ and is negligible far from a wall, so the $\tilde\nu$ of
a wake is convected and diffused, and it is not destroyed. A boundary layer
next to the wake receives fluid with a $\chi$ far above the freestream value,
also where the $\chi$ of that fluid is still below one.
On the flap of Section~III.E, $\chi$ in the boundary layer is $0.5$ to $0.8$
at the incidence where the wake of the main element passes next to it, and
$9\times10^{-4}$ to $0.004$ at the other incidences. It reaches one in less
than one e-fold of growth in place of seven. Where fluid with $\chi>c_{v1}$
enters a boundary layer, the production is already that of SA, and the
amplification of Section~II does not contribute. The transition in the
model is therefore farther upstream next to a wake, the direction of the
measured effect.
